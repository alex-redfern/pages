Vision 2035
Key Takeaways

What EVS believes about the next decade of live production, condensed to what you need in a booth conversation or on a panel. Sixteen cards, about ten minutes end to end. If you have sixty seconds, go straight to the last one.

Who this is for
Leadership Team, sales in customer conversations, and anyone speaking on an IBC panel.
How to use it
Each card carries one message, an approved form of words, and the claim to avoid. Swipe, use arrow keys, or print it.
The rule
Direction is fair game. Dates, codenames and customer names are not. Card 14 explains why.
If you only remember one thing

Why EVS exists in 2035

To enable every organisation to create and monetise live experiences at any scale.

That is deliberately bigger than "we make replay servers" and deliberately bigger than "we provide software-defined infrastructure". It is the sentence everything else on these cards hangs from. If a conversation is going badly, come back to it.

Say
"Our ambition is that anyone producing live content can reach the standard that only the biggest events could afford before."
Avoid
Leading with infrastructure, architecture or the word "platform" as an opener.
Those are how we deliver it. They are not why anyone should care.
The story arc

Hardware, then software
and AI, then platform

Three eras. Each builds on the last. Nothing is abandoned along the way, which is the part customers most need to hear.

Where we came from
Hardware. Purpose-built systems that did not fail, on the biggest events in the world. This is where the trust was earned.
Where we are now
Software and AI. Capability delivered as software that runs where it makes sense, with AI embedded where it measurably helps.
Where this goes
Platform. The orchestration layer across a multi-vendor ecosystem, answerable for the whole production rather than one box in it.
Say
"The reliability that made us a hardware company is the thing we are carrying into the software era, not trading away for it."
Avoid
"We are moving away from hardware."
It reads as abandonment to an installed base that just bought it, and it is not what we mean. See card 10.

Prediction 01

Every production becomes
AI-assisted. Not optionally.

The question stops being whether you use AI and becomes which parts of the workflow still need a human. The honest answer is: the parts that require taste. Every operator works alongside something that watches the whole event and never tires in the eighty-eighth minute.

Say
"AI will not replace the producer. It removes the distance between what a producer imagines and what is possible to deliver live."
Proof you can quote: more than 80,000 highlight clips generated automatically at Paris 2024.
Avoid
Implying AI removes operators, or that any specific AI feature is imminent.
Operator anxiety is real and it is the fastest way to lose a room.
Prediction 02

Content is created once
and published everywhere

Capturing a moment then copying, transcoding and re-versioning it for each destination becomes as dated as tape. One capture serves broadcast, streaming, social, betting, clubs and archive at once, with rights and brand rules travelling with the content rather than being enforced by someone remembering them.

Say
"Ingest once. Every application sees the same frame at the same instant. No duplication, no waiting for a copy to land."
This is shipping today, not a prediction. Safe to demonstrate.
Avoid
Claiming every format and destination is covered today.
The architecture is real; the breadth of destinations is still growing.
Prediction 03

The tide is turning from
boxes to capabilities

Handle this one carefully. Over the decade, production resources become something a customer requests rather than something they own and amortise, and what they are buying becomes the ability to produce. That is the direction, not today's offer. Sell what the customer will be able to do, and be precise about what delivers it now.

The direction
Customers buy capability rather than equipment. A tier-one final and a regional fixture draw on the same pool at different scales, and the difference is a decision rather than a procurement cycle.
Where we are today
Software-defined with guaranteed performance, on our own CPU and GPU hardware. Our Zoom replay application is the example to reach for: genuinely software-defined, and deterministic because of the hardware underneath it.
Say
"You get the flexibility of software with the performance guarantee of hardware built for it. Those two things are usually a trade-off. They should not have to be."
Avoid
Telling customers they can stop buying hardware, or pointing them at off-the-shelf servers from HP, Dell or Intel as the answer today.
Also avoid commercial models, licence structures and pricing direction. Packaging is not settled; route those to the account team.
Prediction 04

Output grows tenfold.
Headcount does not.

This is the arithmetic that forces everything else. If audiences want ten times the content and the team stays the same size, automation stops being an efficiency project and becomes the only way the work gets done. The organisations that win are the ones whose people spend the highest proportion of the day on decisions only a human can make.

Say
"The constraint is no longer what your equipment can do. It is how many hours of human judgement you have available."
Proof you can quote: over 11,000 hours of live production across 4,857 feeds at Paris 2024, in build windows no longer than the Games before.
Avoid
Framing this as headcount reduction.
Customers hear redundancies. The message is more output from the same team, not the same output from fewer.
Prediction 05

Freedom through
orchestration

Customers should not have to depend on a single vendor, or bet their operation on one technology roadmap. They need the freedom to select the best solution for each part of their workflow, while ensuring that everything works together as one coherent system.

The real value therefore lies in the orchestration layer: the layer that removes the complexity of a multi-vendor environment and gives customers the flexibility, interoperability and control to evolve their operations over time.

Say
"You should be free to choose the best tool for each part of your workflow, and still run it as one system. That freedom is what orchestration buys you."
Proof you can quote: orchestration and control across more than 350 device protocols, any vendor, live today.
Avoid
Making this about EVS winning the layer, or naming which partners will be in the ecosystem.
The benefit belongs to the customer. Openness is the message, and a named list makes it sound like a closed club.
Where we disagree with the industry

"Everything becomes
software" is half the story

This is your best panel material, because almost nobody else will say it. Industry perception is moving towards a future that is entirely standard, general-purpose compute. We do not think that is what happens. The market for purpose-built hardware will decline, which is exactly why we are building a software business, but declining is not disappearing.

~5×
The power gap
A purpose-built 1RU multiviewer at 64 in and 8 out draws around 250 watts. A general-purpose equivalent at the same density draws roughly 1,200 to 1,400.
7–10
Years per build
Facilities are specified once and run for a decade. With electricity prices rising, a kilowatt of difference per device is a cost argument before it is a green one.
UHD
Breaks the assumption
General-purpose processing stops scaling gracefully at UHD under complex compression. The gap widens with resolution, it does not close.
Say
"The question was never hardware or software. It is which workloads deserve purpose-built hardware, and that list is shorter in 2035 than today, but it is not empty."
Avoid
Sounding defensive, or naming the competing product behind the power comparison.
Cite our own figure and let them draw the conclusion. Do not quote lifecycle cost in euros; the assumptions are not published.

Vision, strategy and
commitment are different things

Everything on these cards is a point of view about the industry. Almost none of it is a product promise. Keeping the three apart is what lets us be ambitious in public without creating expectations we have not funded.

Vision
A direction we believe in
Where the industry goes and where EVS should play. Say it freely. Never attach a date or a deliverable to it.
Strategy
A choice we have made
An agreed direction with investment behind it, but scope and timing still open. Discuss as intent, never as commitment.
Committed
On the roadmap, with a date
Funded, scoped, scheduled. The only category a customer can plan around, and only via the account team.
Say
"That is where we believe the industry is going and where we intend to lead. What we have committed to deliver is a separate conversation, and I can set that up."
This sentence gets you out of almost any roadmap question without losing credibility.
Never at IBC
Internal project codenames. Launch windows or release timing. Customers named as early adopters of anything unreleased. Anything commercial or financial.
Published customer results are different, and card 13 has the ones you can use. The rule is about unreleased work: it ends up screenshotted. Direction is fair game; specifics are not yours to give.
You will get asked these

The hard questions

"Isn't EVS late to software?"
We were deliberate rather than late. When your equipment runs a World Cup final, moving before the software can carry that reliability is not courage, it is negligence. What we have now is a software stack proven at Games scale rather than a demo. And if the question is really about where our engineering effort goes: 85% of our developers are software developers. Only around 15% work on dedicated hardware.
"Everyone at this show claims to have AI."
Agreed, so ask what it is trained on. Most highlight tools work from a single programme feed. Our customers record every camera, and the unused angles are where the storytelling actually is. That is a data advantage, not a model advantage, and it is much harder to copy.
"Are you abandoning hardware?"
No. We are moving into software everything that should be software, and continuing to build purpose-built hardware for the workloads where power, density and timing still decide the outcome. Card 10 has the numbers if they want them.
"Can a hardware company really become a platform company?"
It is a fair challenge. The honest answer is that the orchestration and control layer is already the fastest-growing part of our portfolio, and it already controls other vendors' equipment. We are not starting from an idea.
"Isn't cloud cheaper?"
Sometimes, and we support it where it is. For elasticity and reach it is compelling. For sustained, latency-critical, high-density work the arithmetic often goes the other way once you count power and egress across a ten-year build. Hybrid is the honest answer.
If you have one minute

The sixty-second version

Our customers are being asked to make more content, for more platforms, with the same team. That pressure only increases. So we think five things become true by 2035: every production is AI-assisted; content is created once and published everywhere; the industry moves from buying boxes to buying capabilities; output grows tenfold while headcount does not; and customers get their freedom back through orchestration rather than by betting on one vendor's roadmap.

EVS is moving from hardware, through software and AI, towards being the orchestration layer that makes a multi-vendor world behave like one system. We are keeping purpose-built hardware where power, density and timing still decide the outcome, because "everything becomes software" is half the story. And the point of all of it is not the technology. It is that every fan gets a feed built for them, and every sport can be produced like the Olympics.

We exist to enable every organisation to create and monetise live experiences at any scale.
